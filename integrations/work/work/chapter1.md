# The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

## Chapter One: The Ledger

The ferry to Skerrow ran twice a week in autumn, and Ewan Bright made it clear, before Mara had even set her suitcase down on the deck, that he was making an exception.

"Thursday's not a Thursday boat," he said. "But your father would've had words with me."

"My father's dead," Mara said.

"Aye. Doesn't stop a man having words." He cast off the line with a flick of his wrist and the *Linnet* shouldered out into the grey water of the sound.

She sat on the bench behind the wheelhouse with her coat buttoned to the throat and watched the mainland shrink. She had not been back in sixteen years. She had left at eighteen with a scholarship and a grudge, and she had kept the grudge in better condition than anything else she owned. It had seen her through university, through her first job and her divorce, through the long, tidy career she had made of auditing other people's claims — water damage, fire, theft, the slow arithmetic of loss. She was good at it. She could tell within a page whether someone was lying.

Skerrow rose out of the sea the way she remembered it: a hump of black rock with grass combed flat by the wind, a scatter of white houses at the harbour, and on the western point, the lighthouse. It was smaller than in her memory. Everything was.

"They automated it in ninety-eight," Ewan said, not turning from the wheel. "You'll know that."

"I know that."

"He stayed on anyway. Board let him keep the cottage. Said it was cheaper than paying someone to check the bulbs." Ewan glanced back at her. "He checked a great deal more than bulbs."

She didn't ask what he meant. She had decided, somewhere on the long train north, that she would ask nothing. She would sign what needed signing, clear what needed clearing, and be on Monday's boat.

---

The keeper's cottage smelled of paraffin, wool, and the particular staleness of a room where an old man had lived alone. Someone — Ewan, she supposed, or one of the harbour wives — had stripped the bed and emptied the fridge. The rest was untouched. A pair of boots by the door, the left heel worn to a slant. A mug on the draining board with a brown ring inside it. On the windowsill, a row of stones she recognised with a jolt: she had collected them herself, aged seven or eight, and lined them up in order of size. He had never moved them.

She stood there longer than she meant to.

Then she did what she knew how to do. She took out a notebook and began an inventory.

Kitchen: serviceable. Furniture: old, solid, worth nothing. Books: tide tables, almanacs, a shelf of paperback westerns gone soft with damp. Radio: working. Clothes: charity. She moved through the rooms methodically, and the methodicalness was a kind of armour, and she knew it was, and she kept it on.

The ledger was in the desk under the window that looked out at the tower.

It was a heavy, clothbound thing, the kind of book once issued to keepers for the official log, its spine reinforced with tape. But when she opened it, it was not the official log. There were no wind readings, no notes on lamp hours or fuel. There were only lines, in her father's cramped, upright hand, each one the same shape:

*14 March 1987. The* Morning Star*, trawler, out of Ullapool. Kept safe.*

*2 April 1987. The* Brannock*, coaster. Kept safe.*

*2 April 1987. Yacht, name unknown, two aboard. Kept safe.*

She turned the pages. Years of them. Decades. Fishing boats and ferries and small private craft, sometimes one a month, sometimes four in a single night. Here and there a line had been struck through and a word written beside it — *Lost* — and those lines had been pressed so hard the nib had torn the paper.

She sat down in his chair without deciding to.

So this was what he had done with himself. Not a man who had chosen a lighthouse over his wife and daughter, she thought, but a man who had chosen a ledger — who had sat at this window night after night keeping score against the sea, crediting himself with every boat that made it home. It was so exactly like him she almost laughed. Her mother had once said that Tobias Quell believed the light would go out if he blinked.

She turned to the back, looking for the end, for the last entry before his heart had stopped on the stairs of the tower three weeks ago.

The handwriting was shakier in the final pages, but the shape of the lines was the same.

*30 September. The* Kittiwake*, creel boat. Kept safe.*

*4 October. The* Ailsa Grace*. Kept safe.*

She frowned. Her father had died on the twenty-eighth of September. She had the certificate in her bag.

She checked the year. This year.

*11 October. The* Brannock *again, the old girl. Kept safe.*

Today was the eighteenth. She read the dates again, slowly, with the careful attention she gave a claim that didn't add up. There would be an explanation. He had been confused at the end; he had written dates wrong; he had been predicting, guessing, filling in the expected traffic of the season the way a bored clerk fills in a timesheet in advance. That was all. She had seen people do stranger things with paperwork.

There was one more line.

The ink looked fresher than the rest, though that was impossible, though she told herself it was the lamplight. It was dated tomorrow.

*19 October. The* Linnet*.*

That was all. No *kept safe*. No *lost*. Just the name of the boat she had crossed on that afternoon, and then a space after it, as if he had lifted his pen and been waiting — had been waiting all this time — to see which word he would need.

Outside, the wind had begun to rise. Across the yard the tower stood pale against the darkening sky, and as she watched, its automated lamp came on: a clean, white, electric sweep, turning and turning over the water, needing no one at all.
